Research & Publications

Swallowing physiology, motor speech disorders, neuromuscular electrical stimulation, and brain imaging — with an emphasis on measurements clinicians can rely on.

Research interests

My research asks a practical question in several different forms: what actually happens in the muscles and the brain when we treat speech and swallowing problems, and how do we measure it reliably? The work spans four connected areas.

Swallowing physiology and dysphagia

How swallowing works, what goes wrong after stroke or neurological illness, and how the throat and larynx respond during treatment — including work on laryngeal vestibule opening and how healthy people adapt to stimulation while swallowing.

Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES)

Surface electrical stimulation applied to the tongue, lips, face, and the muscles under the chin. My studies look at whether it changes muscle strength, how much stimulation people can comfortably tolerate over a course of treatment, and what the evidence does and does not yet support.

Brain imaging with fNIRS

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy is a safe, wearable way of watching blood flow in the brain while someone speaks, reads, or listens. I use it to compare brain activation in typically developing individuals and in people with dyslexia and other disabilities, and to study what happens in the cortex during facial NMES.

Measurement and early identification

Building tools clinicians can trust: normative values for tongue strength in children, reliability testing across different measurement devices, and nationally standardized screening instruments for identifying young children with disabilities, including autism.

Measuring tongue and lip strength

Much of my measurement research relies on a deceptively simple tool: a small air-filled bulb that the tongue or lips press against a sensor, which reads the pressure in kilopascals. The hard questions are whether different devices give comparable readings, and whether different examiners get the same result from the same placement.

Three devices, one measurement

A study now under review compares maximum tongue pressure in healthy adults in the United Arab Emirates across three instruments, working toward device-specific normative values — because a reading taken on one device cannot simply be assumed to mean the same thing on another.

Six bulb placements

A second study under review examines how consistently different examiners obtain the same pressure reading at six placements of the IOPI bulb — two on the tongue, two at the lips, one against the cheek, and one between the molars. The two tongue placements are illustrated below.
