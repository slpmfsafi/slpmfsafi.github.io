Teaching & Mentorship

Doctoral, master’s, and undergraduate teaching in speech-language pathology and special education since 2010, alongside supervised research that has reached publication.

My teaching has spanned six institutions: Howard University, the University of the District of Columbia, King Saud University, United Arab Emirates University, Fontbonne University, and Qatar University. Alongside degree courses, I teach postgraduate continuing education for practicing clinicians.

Teaching philosophy

“By learning you will teach; by teaching you will learn.” That Latin proverb has followed me through every classroom I have taught in, from Washington, DC to Riyadh, Al Ain, St. Paul, and Doha. My aim in each of them has been the same: a learning environment that is active and evidence-based, whether my audience is a lecture hall of undergraduates, a small group of graduate clinicians, or a patient in a clinic. Over the years my style has settled into something practical, experiential, and occasionally humorous, because a saying I have carried since my clinical training still holds: tell me and I will forget; show me and I may not remember; involve me and I will understand.

Three commitments shape how I teach, and they are linked. The first is respect for the learner. I begin every course by finding out what my students already know and think, and I bring them into planning the syllabus early, so the course is built on their starting point rather than mine. Respect also means honesty about expectations, admitting my own mistakes and correcting them quickly, and making the classroom a safe place to experiment and take chances. The second is an appreciation of different learning styles. Any classroom holds many kinds of learners, so I offer many routes in. In my doctoral seminar on language and literacy, each student prepares and leads a session on a major topic, with an outline and discussion questions for the class. In assistive technology, and in anatomy and physiology, students work through hands-on worksheets in groups. Wherever I can, I get students out of their seats and into the laboratory, whether the Special Education Lab or the Neuroscience of Education Lab. Examinations follow the same principle: I want them to reflect understanding rather than memorization, so I use a variety of tools, including short tests and quizzes, hands-on worksheets, written reports, and structured discussion.

The third is continuous refinement. Every teaching session is an experiment, and I treat student evaluations as data: I read them every semester, take the suggestions seriously, and change the course. That habit is why I built a hybrid course on Blackboard in 2019, a year before the pandemic made online teaching compulsory, and why, when the lockdown came, I could move every course fully online with a virtual lab, in-session polls, and more discussion to keep students engaged. It is also why I share my course materials with colleagues who teach the same subjects and train new faculty on the tools. My goal in all of this is that students take responsibility for their own learning, at whatever level they enter, and discover their own ability to become good clinicians.

Commitment to inclusive education

I have lived on four continents and taught in two very different academic cultures, the United States and the Gulf, working in Arabic and English with students whose backgrounds, languages, and expectations of a classroom differed widely. That record, more than any statement, is where my commitment to inclusion lives. At United Arab Emirates University I redesigned the undergraduate Introduction to Rehabilitation course around the World Health Organization’s World Report on Disability, so that a class of Emirati women training in special education could talk openly and honestly about disability, rehabilitation, inclusion, and bias in their own society. I advocated for the admission of students with disabilities to undergraduate programs, helped draft the university’s first policy on it, and reviewed a proposed national admission policy for students with disabilities for the UAE’s higher-education authority. In the classroom itself, inclusion is a daily practice rather than a claim: making sure every student’s contribution is heard, drawing out those who are shy or junior, and supporting collaboration across differences. I was fortunate to have role models and support at every stage of my own education, and I try to be that for students who are, in one way or another, different from the room they walk into.

For prospective students & mentees

My approach to mentorship

Most of the students I have supervised arrived with a clinical question, not a research topic. A speech-language pathologist who wondered whether the tongue-strength readings she took on children were reliable. A special-education teacher who wanted to know whether the picture-exchange system she used every day actually worked in Emirati classrooms. A doctoral student who suspected that dyslexia would show up in a child’s brain activity during reading. My job was to help each of them turn that question into a study that could answer it — and then to make sure the answer reached the field rather than staying in a thesis. Four of my five completed master’s students, and my doctoral student, co-authored their thesis work in indexed international journals: PLOS ONE, BMC Psychology, Advances in Autism, Frontiers in Education, and the Journal of the Neurological Sciences. Several of them maintain their own pages describing the work in their own words, which I link from My Students’ Pages as the best evidence of what supervision with me looks like.

The way I supervise follows the way I teach. I start with what you already know, which is usually more than you think, and I bring you into planning the project early so that it is built on your question rather than mine. I am honest about expectations and about my own mistakes, and I expect the same honesty back — the fastest way to a good study is to be wrong early and correct it quickly. In the laboratory at United Arab Emirates University, my graduate students worked as paid research assistants with their own workstations and hands-on training on the fNIRS and NMES equipment, and they presented their work at international conferences before they graduated. I also read and comment on instruments, surveys, and manuscripts for students who are not formally mine, because a question worth asking deserves a second pair of eyes. In 2023 the College of Medicine and Health Sciences at UAEU recognized this with its Best Mentor in Health Sciences Award, which I mention not as a credential but as evidence that the approach works for students.

What I look for

I am not looking for a finished researcher. I am looking for a curious clinician or educator who has noticed something in practice that does not quite add up and wants to find out why. Beyond that, a few things matter more than grades. A genuine question of your own — even a rough one — because I can help you sharpen a question but I cannot supply the wanting. The willingness to be wrong in front of me and change course without taking it personally; that is where the learning is. Patience for measurement, because much of this work is the unglamorous business of taking the same reading again and again until we trust it. The habit of finishing what you start, since a study that is never written up helps no one. And some comfort with numbers, or at least the readiness to learn them — I will teach you the statistics you need, but you must be willing to sit with them.

Directions I am open to supervising

  • Tongue-pressure measurement and normative data — reliability across devices and examiners, and reference values for children and adults in the Gulf, using the IOPI and comparable instruments.
  • Applications of neuromuscular electrical stimulation — submental and facial placements, tolerance and comfort across a course of treatment, and what the stimulation does to the cortex.
  • fNIRS studies of reading and language — cortical activation during reading, listening, and speaking in typically developing children and in children with dyslexia or other learning differences.
  • Early identification of communication disorders — screening instruments for infants and young children, validated for local populations and usable by non-specialists.
  • Communication interventions in autism — picture-exchange systems, digital social stories, voice-assistant technology, and how families and teachers actually use them.

How to reach me

Write to me at slpmfsafi@gmail.com with the subject line Prospective student, and include four things: a short account of your background — your degree, your clinical or teaching experience, and where you did it; a paragraph on the question that interests you, in your own words, however unformed; your CV as an attachment; and your timeline — when you hope to start, at what level, and whether you have a program or funding in view. That is enough for me to reply properly. I read every message, and I would rather receive a rough idea now than a polished one next year.
